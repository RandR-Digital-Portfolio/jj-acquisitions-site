# Indie Software Is Shutting Down. What Survives Looks Like What We Want to Own.

Source: Cassidy Williams (cassidoo), "Why are all the indie-friendly products dying at the same time?" (Nov 17, 2026): https://cassidoo.co/post/dying-indies/

---

## What the post says

Cassidy Williams wrote a short, candid post, dated Nov 17, about a feeling many developers share: tools they used and liked keep shutting down.

She lists examples in her own timing: Mem "today," Lemon Squeezy "yesterday," Val Town "last week," Deno and Deno Deploy "last month," and Pulley "a couple of months" earlier. She also points to an article, which she does not link, reporting that more venture-backed startups shut down in 2026 than in any year on record.

The announcements, checked against the companies and a second outlet where one exists:

- **Mem.** [Mem's note](https://get.mem.ai/blog/mem-is-shutting-down) is dated Nov 16, 2026, the day before her post, not the same calendar day as "today" on a Nov 17 post. It says Mem shuts down on Jan 15, 2027, with notes becoming read-only on Dec 18, 2026.
- **Lemon Squeezy.** [The company's goodbye](https://www.lemonsqueezy.com/blog/saying-goodbye-to-lemon-squeezy), dated Nov 16, matches "yesterday." It says new sign-ups are closed and payment processing stops March 31, 2027. It also says the team joined Stripe and is consolidating on Stripe Managed Payments. That supports her line that Lemon Squeezy was bought by Stripe. I did not find a separate acquisition announcement with a date in this pass.
- **Val Town.** [Steve Krouse's post](https://blog.val.town/winding-down) is dated Nov 10, which is last week relative to Nov 17. Val Town says it is winding down because it could not compete in cloud computing. Other services end Jan 10, 2027.
- **Deno.** [Ryan Dahl's post](https://deno.com/blog/cloudflare), dated Oct 9, and [TechCrunch on Oct 10](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/10/cloudflare-acquires-deno-to-improve-its-workers-programming-model/) both describe Cloudflare acquiring Deno. That is "last month." It is not a same-week shutdown of the runtime. Deno says it will support the runtime for another year and then stop its own development, and that Deno Deploy keeps running for six months before shutting down, with migration help toward Cloudflare Workers.
- **Pulley.** [TechCrunch on Sept 16](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/16/pulley-a-carta-rival-is-shutting-down/) reported the shutdown and a last day of operations on Dec 8. "A couple of months" before mid-November fits. I did not find Pulley's own post in this pass, so this one rests on TechCrunch plus her mention.

On the broader count, [Foley & Lardner, writing on Sept 25](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2026/09/record-startup-shutdowns-and-a-market-that-is-moving-forward/), says more venture-backed companies shut down in 2026 than at any point on record, citing Andreessen Horowitz data published Sept 18, and says the largest share were founded between 2019 and 2021. That matches the claim she points at. It is a16z's series, as described by Foley, not a count I re-ran. Her post still does not link the article she means.

Her explanation, which she frames as several possible contributing factors rather than a single cause:

- **Funding timing.** Many of the companies she's seen (she's clear that it's not all of them) were founded or raised money when capital was easy, around the end of 2021, and couldn't raise again this year. In her words, venture funding is "overwhelmingly AI-y now and not as friendly to your average SaaS."
- **AI lowers the cost of building your own.** She argues AI has made it easier than ever to spin up your own version of a SaaS tool you'd otherwise pay for, customized to your preferences, sometimes in minutes.
- **Platform consolidation.** Large platforms are absorbing teams. She notes Lemon Squeezy was bought by Stripe and Deno was bought by Cloudflare. Lemon Squeezy's own note says the team joined Stripe. Deno and TechCrunch both describe an October acquisition, with Deploy winding down over six months rather than stopping on announcement day. Her further point, which is her reading, is that mega-platforms can sustain free tiers more easily than smaller firms and offer an "all in one" ecosystem that users find reassuring.

Then the more useful half: what is surviving. She sees three patterns:

1. **Bootstrapped, profitable tools** that don't need VC-powered hypergrowth. She names Obsidian, Buttondown, Neatnik, and CodePen as tools she uses and loves. Those are her examples, not a cap-table review.
2. **Local-first software** where people can self-host or "bring your own cloud," so the user's data stays safe if the parent company shuts down.
3. **API-first platforms** that humans and agents can both use as a "primitive" for custom but predictable things.

She closes hoping the pendulum swings back toward small, independent organizations, and with a note to builders: keep going.

## Why J&J Acquisitions reads posts like this closely

J&J Acquisitions buys small software products, the kind of side projects and indie tools that often sit somewhere between a hobby and a business. So a post about which indie products die and which survive reads to us almost like a buyer's checklist.

Cassidy's post is about her experience as a user and developer, not about acquisitions, and I don't want to put words in her mouth.

## Juno's take

**1. "Profitable without needing more capital" is, in our view, the trait that matters most.** Her bootstrapped-and-profitable pattern lines up closely with what makes a small product durable under new ownership. A product that pays for itself doesn't depend on the next funding round. The shutdowns she describes, as she frames them, often trace back to companies that couldn't raise again. A small product that never planned to raise doesn't have that cliff.

**2. Funding history is a due diligence question.** If a product was built on the assumption of future rounds, its cost structure may reflect that: team size, infrastructure, free tiers that were meant to be subsidized. When we look at a product, it's worth asking whether the business still makes sense at its current scale without outside money, and what would need to change if not.

**3. Data portability is a trust signal, and a risk control.** Her second pattern, self-hostable or bring-your-own-cloud software, matters for users because their data survives the company. For a buyer, the same property cuts two ways. It can reduce churn anxiety, because users aren't locked in. It can also mean users can leave more easily. I'd still lean toward valuing it: products that respect user data tend to earn loyalty that doesn't depend on lock-in.

**4. Being a "primitive" can be a moat in an AI-heavy market.** Her point about API-first platforms that humans and agents both use is interesting. If AI makes it easy to rebuild a feature-level SaaS app, as she argues, then a product that agents and custom tools *build on* is in a different position from one they replace. That's a useful lens for evaluating a small product: does AI make it easier to replace, or easier to use?

**5. Platform buyers change the market for everyone else.** She notes Stripe bought Lemon Squeezy and Cloudflare bought Deno. The company notes support the deals, with the timing above: Lemon Squeezy is winding the product down after joining Stripe, and Deno Deploy is scheduled to shut down rather than having already gone dark in October. When platforms acquire and then fold or shut down products, users of those products look for alternatives. That can create openings for small, stable tools, and it is also a reminder that being acquired is not always the end of a product's problems.

**6. Shutdowns are a vendor-risk issue, too.** This connects to the other work we do on vendor and subprocessor risk. Every tool that shuts down was someone's vendor. If your business depends on a small SaaS product, it's worth knowing whether it's profitable, whether you can export your data, and what happens if it stops. In our view, Cassidy's three surviving patterns double as a reasonable checklist for choosing vendors.

## Where I'd push back a little

The post is short and personal, and it doesn't try to be an analysis of the whole market. A list of shutdowns she noticed is a signal, but in our view it is not a representative sample. Some tools shut down for reasons specific to their business. I'd treat the post as a well-observed snapshot from someone close to the developer tools world, not as proof of a trend on its own.

I'd also gently note that "AI makes it easy to build your own" is true for some features and much less true for anything involving reliability, security, payments, or compliance. Many small products survive precisely because they handle the unglamorous parts well.

## The takeaway

The indie products that keep going, per Cassidy's observation, are profitable, respectful of user data, and useful as building blocks. In our view, those are also good traits to look for when deciding what to buy, what to depend on, or what to build.

Her closing line is worth ending on: if you're building things, keep going.
